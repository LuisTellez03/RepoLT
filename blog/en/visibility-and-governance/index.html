Asset Management Visibility Reliability

From Visibility to Governance: When Asset Management Becomes Actionable

calendar_today September 27, 2026
schedule 3 min read

In the previous post, I stated that IT asset management begins with knowing and verifying what exists in the technological environment. Chapters 3 and 7 of Rethinking Information Technology Asset Management, by Jeremy L. Boerger, take this idea a step further: discovering assets is not enough if there is no team, rules, and processes to keep that information useful over time.

Chapter 3 focuses on discovery and validation. Its main idea is that we should not assume a record is true just because it appears in a tool or an inventory sheet. Boerger proposes contrasting three sources of evidence: electronic inventory, visual inspection, and supporting documentation. By comparing these sources, we can identify matches, but also discrepancies that require investigation. An asset visible to a security tool but absent from the official inventory could be an unregistered device, an incorrectly retired asset, a failure in the onboarding process, or even a false positive. In all cases, it represents information that we should not ignore.

This stance is particularly relevant for cybersecurity. If we do not know an asset, we can hardly confirm if it has a supported operating system version, applied hardening configurations, current patches, or security monitoring. Visibility must be understood as a control capability: it allows us to identify the technological surface that must be protected and relate each asset to the service or ecosystem it supports.

I have been able to observe this relationship in an organization where I was responsible for communication equipment. The challenge was not just inventorying hundreds of network devices, but maintaining a useful relationship between each piece of equipment and the ecosystem it served. We established a policy so that every device was registered before going into production. The record included structured information: standardized name, device type, serial number, brand, model, physical location, IP address, operating system version, served ecosystem, and asset owner. Configuration backups were also maintained.

This scheme turned the inventory into an operational tool. In the event of a failure, we could identify more quickly which ecosystem could be affected, which equipment was responsible for a connection point, and who should participate in the response. At the same time, having operating system versions and backed-up configurations facilitated integration with cybersecurity systems and the automation of compliance validations, including device hardening.
