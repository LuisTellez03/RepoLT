IT Asset Management: The Starting Point to Reduce Chaos

calendar_today September 23, 2026
schedule 3 min read

As I began to delve into IT asset management, I understood that it is not just about making a list of computers, servers, or licenses. It is about knowing the technological environment with enough certainty to be able to operate, protect, and improve it. The initial chapters by Martyn Hobbs and Jeremy L. Boerger agree on an idea that seems essential to me: when an organization does not know precisely what it owns, where it is located, who uses it, and how it relates to other elements, IT operations tend to become a constant reaction to incidents.

Hobbs describes this scenario as a type of operational chaos. A service fails, an attempt is made to restore it, but it is not clearly known which devices, applications, or dependencies are involved. In that context, every incident seems isolated, investigating the root cause becomes slow, and technological changes can generate new interruptions. Therefore, asset management and configuration management should not be seen as secondary administrative tasks: they provide the information that connects incident, problem, and change management. A CMDB is only useful if it helps answer practical questions, such as which server supports a critical application, which people would be affected by a failure, or what risk a planned change would have.

The reading has also helped me relate ITAM to cybersecurity. It is not possible to properly manage vulnerabilities in assets that have not been identified. An unregistered piece of equipment, a forgotten virtual machine, or software installed without control can be left out of updates, monitoring, and security policies. Thus, asset visibility is not only an operational advantage: it is a condition for knowing the technological surface that must be protected.

However, Boerger adds an important clarification: having data is not enough; the data must be reliable and verifiable. A report stating that there is a certain number of laptops or licenses does not constitute, by itself, a truth. We must ask ourselves where the information comes from, when it was validated, and what evidence supports it. A serial number observed in a physical audit, a network log, an invoice, an assignment to a user, or a destruction certificate can provide evidence. By contrasting several sources, the organization reduces the risk of making decisions based on outdated or incomplete records.

This idea seems especially useful to me because it avoids two common mistakes. The first is relying only on purchasing documents and administrative movements: that an asset has been acquired does not prove that it is still in use, that it remains in the organization, or that it has a secure configuration. The second is relying only on periodic financial inventories: these can support cost control but do not always describe the current technical reality. Effective asset management requires combining processes, inventories, documentation, discovery tools, and continuous validation.
